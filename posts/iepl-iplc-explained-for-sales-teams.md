If you sell international connectivity, you will see two terms constantly: IEPL and IPLC. If you do not run the network yourself, it helps to understand what they mean before you write about them, because buyers who search them are usually in the middle of a real decision.

## What the terms stand for

IEPL stands for International Ethernet Private Line. IPLC stands for International Private Leased Circuit. Both describe a dedicated connection between two locations in different countries. "Dedicated" means the capacity is reserved for one customer, rather than shared across the public internet.

In practice the terms are used loosely, and suppliers describe their products in slightly different ways. IEPL generally points to an Ethernet-based service, and IPLC is the older and broader term for a leased circuit. If you are writing about a specific product, check the exact specification with your technical team before you publish anything.

## Why buyers search these terms

Someone typing IEPL or IPLC into a search engine already knows roughly what they want. They are comparing suppliers, checking how a route is delivered or trying to understand the difference between two quotes. That makes the terms useful to understand, even if you never run an ad on them.

Searches like these tend to be informational and specific, which is why a plain explanation can earn attention even before a buyer is ready to ask for a quote. We would not claim that proves demand for any one supplier. It does suggest buyers look for clear answers.

## How to write about them clearly

Start with the buyer's question, not your product. A good page answers three things in order: what the service is, what to compare between suppliers, and what the buyer should ask for.

Useful things to compare include the route, the bandwidth, the term of the contract, how faults are handled and who to call when something breaks. Say which of these you can answer and which depend on the order. Do not invent performance numbers. If you cannot show it, leave it out.

Keep the language plain. Engineers will forgive a simple explanation. Procurement will not forgive a confusing one.
