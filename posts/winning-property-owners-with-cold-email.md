Property owners, asset managers and developers get a steady stream of agent outreach. Most of it promises great results and asks for a meeting. The ones that get a reply tend to be quieter and more specific.

## Pick an asset type and a place

Real estate is local. An office landlord, an industrial owner and a retail owner care about different things, and a message for one rarely suits another. Choose one asset type and one area, and write the list around it. A message that says you handle leasing for mid-sized industrial sites in one region is easy to place.

## Say how you work, not how great you are

Claims about "proven results" are hard to check and easy to ignore. Describe what you do: how you take on a vacancy, what the owner receives before signing anything and who they will deal with. A written plan for one site is a concrete, low-risk offer.

## Leave out deals, prices and yields

Do not mention transactions you cannot document, prices or yields. Property rules differ by place and misleading claims can create legal problems. If you cannot show it, leave it out. This is marketing practice, not legal advice, so check local rules with your own adviser.

## Make the ask small

A meeting is a big ask for a first message. A short call to compare approaches for one specific site is smaller and easier to accept.

## Use public information to qualify

Ownership records, listings and news can show who owns a site and whether space is available. Use them to decide who to write to and to mention one true detail. Do not guess at details you do not know.

## Follow up over months

Mandates and projects move slowly. A sequence that adds something each time, such as a market note for that area or asset type, works better than repeating the first message.
