Software buyers get more cold email than almost anyone. They have learned to delete anything that sounds like a pitch. If you sell software or an AI product, the way through is not a cleverer opening line. It is a clearer message.

## Start with the problem, not the product

Most outreach starts with the product: what it is, what it does, how many features it has. The reader cares about none of that until they know you understand their problem. Open with the problem in their words, such as the manual task, the slow process or the spreadsheet that keeps breaking.

If you cannot say the problem in one sentence, you are not ready to write the email.

## Give one proof

One concrete, checkable detail does more than five claims. That could be what the product does on a real document, where it runs, or what it integrates with. Avoid invented numbers and avoid customer names you do not have permission to use. If you do not have a result you can stand behind, show the product instead.

## Do not hide the awkward questions

Buyers of software, and AI in particular, usually ask about data: where it goes, who can see it and whether it trains a model. If your product has a clear answer, put one line of it in the email or on the page you link to. It signals you have heard the question before and builds more trust than a claim that you are secure.

## Ask for a small step

"Book a demo" is a large ask. A better one is a short demo on one of their own documents or workflows, or a link to a two minute walkthrough. The first email is there to earn a reply.

## Pick one segment at a time

A message that works for a finance team will not work for a logistics team. Choose one kind of company with one clear problem and test it before widening. A narrow first segment tells you more in two weeks than a broad one does in two months. The process is covered in our [cold outreach guide](/blog/cold-outreach-guide/).

## Follow up with something new

A reply often comes on the second or third message. Each one should add something: another use case, a short example, a useful link. Repeating "just checking in" teaches the reader to ignore you. See [lead follow-up: how fast, how often, when to stop](/blog/lead-follow-up/).
