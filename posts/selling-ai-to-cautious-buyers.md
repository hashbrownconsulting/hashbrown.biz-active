Many buyers are curious about AI and cautious about buying it. They worry about where their data goes, whether the output can be trusted and who is accountable if something goes wrong. If you sell an AI product, your job is to answer those worries before they become reasons to wait.

## Answer the data question first

The most common question is simple: what happens to our data? Be ready to say where it is stored, who can see it, whether it is used to train a model and how it is deleted. If an answer depends on the plan or the contract, say so plainly. A clear "here is what we do" is worth more than a promise that you take security seriously.

Put the answer where the buyer will find it, such as a short page linked from your outreach, not buried in a policy document.

## Be honest about limits

AI output can be wrong, and buyers know it. A product that says what it does well, where it needs a person to check and how mistakes are caught earns trust faster than one that implies it is always right. If there is a task it should not be used for, say so.

## Offer a pilot, not a leap

A small, time-boxed pilot on real work lowers the risk of saying yes. Agree on a narrow use case, a short period, what the buyer will provide and what counts as success before you start. A pilot with unclear success criteria ends in "interesting, let's keep talking", which is not a decision.

## Know who else will weigh in

The person who likes the product is often not the one who approves it. Security, IT, legal and the budget owner may all have questions. Give your champion short, accurate material they can forward, covering what it does, how data is handled and what the pilot involves.

## Skip claims you cannot support

Superlatives and unsupported numbers make cautious buyers more cautious. Describe what the product does today, show it working and let the buyer judge.
