After eighty days live, this site has 95 search impressions and one click. That is normal for a new domain, and the useful number is not the click. It is that impressions went from under one a day to nearly five a day the week we published two posts aimed at specific searches, while our average position fell from 7.6 to 62.

Here is what that means, and what three client builds this week added to it.

## What the data actually says

Between 30 July and 17 October, Google showed the site 95 times and sent one visitor, in August, to the homepage. Of those impressions, 91 were on desktop and 43 were in the United States. Before 8 October, we averaged about 0.7 impressions a day. From 8 October, when the two keyword posts went live, we averaged 4.8.

The two posts account for most of it. The follow-up email post has 24 impressions at an average position of 56. The cold email timing post has 21 at 77. Search results pages hold ten listings, so a position in the 50s is the sixth page and the 70s is the eighth. Nobody clicks there, which is why the click count did not move.

## Why average position got worse when we did more right

Before the two posts, we showed up for almost nothing, so our average position described almost nothing. A handful of impressions at a good position is a small, flattering number. Once we published content aimed at real searches, we started appearing for queries we could not appear for at all, and we appeared at the back, because that is where a new page on a new domain starts.

The average fell because the set it described got bigger and harder. If you only watch average position, you will read progress as decline. Impressions per day and the number of distinct queries are the leading indicators. Position by page is the one to improve.

> An average over almost nothing looks great. An average over something real looks like where you actually stand.

## The competition column is not about rankings

The keyword research behind the follow-up post showed a competition score near zero and an estimated 9,900 searches a month. We read that as an open field. It was not. That column measures how many advertisers bid on the term, not how hard it is to rank for it organically. The post sits at position 67 for its main query, which means 66 other results are ahead of it.

The volume number was real and the competition number was a different measurement under a misleading name. Check who already ranks before you decide a term is open.

## Three jobs this week, one lesson each

**A claims audit across a large marketing site.** More than a hundred pages, and one company fact stated three different ways: as a running count of years in some places and a founding year in others. A running count is wrong the day after it is written. We scanned every page for stale claims, banned wording, and numbers that disagreed with each other, then fixed them in one pass. The lesson: every fact on a site is a liability multiplied by the number of pages it appears on. Some of it also sat in structured data that the page editor cannot reach, so the visible copy was right and the hidden copy was not.

**A stock update for an online store.** New stock numbers came in from a supplier sheet. We compared them against the live store first, and only eight variants had actually changed. We applied those, checked each on the live store, kept a rollback file, and flagged 26 items in the sheet that did not exist on the store instead of silently skipping them. The lesson: showing a product that is out of stock, or hiding one that is back, is a marketing error with a stock room cause. Diff before you change anything.

**A product page rebuilt with real screenshots.** We replaced hand-built mockups with real product screenshots, and they sell better. They also carried things nobody had chosen to say: another brand's name, a named individual, a third party's name, and a badge making a claim that the site's own rules forbid. We flagged it and asked instead of treating the page as finished. The lesson: review a screenshot the way you review copy, because every pixel in it is a claim.

## What we are watching instead of clicks

Impressions per day, distinct queries, and position by page, not the site average. At this age the job is to get the right pages in front of the right searches and then move them up from the back, which is slow work and mostly depends on the content being better than what already ranks. We will keep reporting the numbers as they are.
