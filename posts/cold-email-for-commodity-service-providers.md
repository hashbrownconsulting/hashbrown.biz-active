Commodity markets have a particular problem with cold email. Unsolicited offers to sell or buy cargo are a common scam approach, so experienced buyers treat almost all of them as suspect. If you sell a legitimate service to traders and producers, you are fighting that reputation before the reader gets to your second sentence.

## Sell the service, not the trade

Inspection, testing, shipping, storage and trade software are services with a clear scope. Say what yours is. Do not mention prices, volumes or availability of any commodity, and do not offer to find buyers or sellers. We do not write that kind of message, and we do not broker or arrange trades.

## Make yourself easy to verify

Buyers will check who you are. A short message that names the company, where it operates and one checkable fact, such as a location or accreditation, helps. Link to a page with company details, named contacts and a clear scope. A form-only page with no names reads as a risk.

## Keep the claim small

Say what you do and where you do it. Avoid superlatives and avoid anything that sounds like a promise of speed or savings that you cannot put in a contract.

## Ask for a small step

A short call to compare scope and turnaround for one route or one site is a smaller ask than a meeting. The aim of the first message is a reply from a real person.

## Protect your own sender reputation

Messages that look like trade offers are reported as spam and can damage the domain you rely on for everything else. A short, plain, service-focused message protects deliverability. See [why most outbound fails before the first email sends](/blog/hello-outbound/).

## Be careful with compliance

Trade, sanctions and advertising rules vary by country. This is marketing practice, not legal advice, so check what applies to you with a qualified adviser. For the general method, read our [cold outreach guide](/blog/cold-outreach-guide/).
