Almost every company that ships already has a logistics provider. That changes what a cold email should try to do. You are rarely asking someone to drop their provider. You are asking to be considered for a lane, a service or a trial.

## Name one lane or one service

"Full-service logistics solutions" says nothing. "Full and part loads from Shanghai to Sydney with customs handled in house" tells the reader whether they should keep reading. Pick the lane or service you are strongest in and lead with it. If you have several, write several emails for several segments.

## Be the second option

Shippers like having a backup. A second provider reduces risk when the first is late, full or expensive. Say that plainly: you would like to be considered as a second option for one lane. That is a much easier yes than a full switch, and many long relationships start that way.

## Write for the person who reviews providers

In many companies it is a procurement or supply chain lead, sometimes with operations involved. They care about reliability, price against what they pay now, and how much work it is to onboard you. Make the next step small: a short call to compare lane pricing, or a rate sheet for one route.

## Leave out claims you cannot back up

Transit times, on-time rates and service levels get checked. Quote only what you can put in a contract. Skip "industry leading" and "best rates". If you do not know their current setup, do not guess.

## Time it to the review window

Many buyers review providers on a schedule, such as annually or around a peak season. A follow-up sequence that adds something new each time, such as a new lane or a relevant update, can land when the review starts.
