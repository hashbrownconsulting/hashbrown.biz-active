Commodity markets attract scams, so experienced traders and producers are careful with anyone new. A legitimate service firm, such as an inspector, shipper, storage operator or software provider, should expect to be checked. Making that easy is part of selling.

This is a general picture, not compliance or legal advice.

## Who you are

The first check is identity. Buyers look for a registered company, a real address, named people and a web presence that matches. A site with no names and a form-only contact reads as a risk. Put company details, locations and named contacts where they are easy to find.

## What you actually do

Buyers want a clear scope: what the service covers, where it operates, which standards it follows and how results are reported. Be specific about what is and is not included. Avoid vague claims that could describe anyone.

## Whether others vouch for you

References and accreditations carry weight. Use only what you hold and can document, and only with permission. If you cannot name clients, say what kind of work you do and where, and offer to share details on a call where appropriate.

## How you handle risk

Traders think about delays, disputes and liability. A short, plain description of how reports are issued, how problems are handled and what your terms say answers a lot. Check the wording with your own adviser.

## What not to say

Do not mention prices, volumes or availability of any commodity, and do not offer to find buyers or sellers. Offers like that are the pattern scam emails use, and they will get you ignored and reported.

## What this means for outreach

Name the service, where it operates, one checkable fact and a small next step, and link to a page with company details and a named contact. See [cold email for commodity service providers](/blog/cold-email-for-commodity-service-providers/) for the writing side.
