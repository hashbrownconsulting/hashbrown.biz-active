Commercial energy buyers, from factory owners to property managers, have heard large savings claims many times. Cold email that repeats them tends to be ignored. Email that offers a clear service and a low-risk first step usually does better.

## Leave out the savings promise

Savings depend on the site: its load, roof, tariff and operating hours. A number in a cold email, before you have seen any of that, is a guess and the reader knows it. Say what the service is and what the first step involves. Share numbers once you have looked at the site.

## Offer a low-risk first step

A feasibility review, an energy audit or a roof assessment is easier to say yes to than a quote. Say what the buyer gets and what it costs them, in one or two lines. If the first step is free, say so. If it is not, say what it is.

## Segment by site type

A factory, a warehouse and an office building have different needs, so one message will not fit all three. Pick one site type and one service, and write to the person who feels the cost: an operations, facilities or finance lead.

## Be careful with technical claims

System sizes, performance and approvals are things buyers or their advisers will check. Quote only what you can put in a proposal. Leave regulatory, grid connection and tariff advice to qualified advisers.

## Expect a long cycle

Energy projects involve finance, operations and sometimes approvals. A sequence that adds something each time, such as a relevant case type or a seasonal reason to act, works better than repeating the first message.
